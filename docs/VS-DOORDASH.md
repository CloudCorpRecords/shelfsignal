# ShelfSignal vs the delivery apps

The delivery apps are not a discovery problem. They are a toll booth.

| | DoorDash / Uber Eats model | ShelfSignal |
|---|---|---|
| Who owns the listing | The platform | The merchant |
| What the merchant pays | 15-30% of every order, plus ads to rank | $100 flat, one time, to get listed |
| Who owns the customer | The platform | The merchant (agents send people to the store) |
| Menu truth | Rented, scraped, or stale partner feeds | Merchant-submitted, dated, decays when stale |
| Stock truth | None. "Unavailable" discovered after ordering | Scored likelihood with reasons, before anyone moves |
| How agents reach it | No open protocol; closed apps | Open MCP server, any agent can query |
| Ranking | Pay to play | Validation tier and freshness only |
| The store's tablet farm | Yes | No |

## The different vertical

ShelfSignal is not a delivery company and does not want to be one. Delivery is logistics; this is **agent commerce infrastructure**: the verified local inventory layer that every assistant, concierge agent, travel agent, and neighborhood bot will need before it can act in the physical city.

The fight is not "deliver food faster." The fight is: **when an AI agent looks for something in the real world, whose index does it read, and who gets paid?** If the answer stays "the delivery apps' closed, ad-ranked, stock-blind index," local stores keep paying rent to be found in their own neighborhood. ShelfSignal makes the index open, merchant-owned, validated, and cheap enough that a bodega can afford to be found by machines.

## What $100 buys that 30% never did

- A permanent, agent-readable listing the merchant controls.
- A validation receipt that makes agents trust and recommend the store.
- Stock signals that bring in people who actually find what they came for.
- Zero per-order tax on being successful.
